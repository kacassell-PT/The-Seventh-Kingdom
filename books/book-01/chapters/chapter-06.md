# CHAPTER SIX
The Shepherd

From The Testament of Light – Fragment VI

The Shepherd cannot carry both the staff and the sword.

One gathers the flock.

The other scatters it.

The Kingdom will choose shepherds.

The kingdoms of men will always choose soldiers.

The mountains remembered things that cities forgot.

They remembered silence.

Not the engineered silence of insulated apartments or noise-canceling architecture.

Real silence.

The kind that made a man hear his own heart.

Marcus Reed preferred it.

He had lived on Redemption Ridge for four years, three months, and seventeen days.

He counted because counting reminded him he was still alive.

Every morning began the same way.

Before sunrise.

Before coffee.

Before work.

Before memory could speak louder than truth.

He knelt beside the narrow bed he had built from reclaimed pine.

No holograms.

No digital assistant.

No Harmony Reflection.

Only worn floorboards and a weathered Bible whose leather cover had nearly separated from the binding.

His prayer never changed.

"Lord..."

He bowed his head.

"...keep me from becoming the weapon I once was."

Nothing more.

No speeches.

No bargaining.

No promises.

Just surrender.

Outside, dawn slowly painted the Montana sky in shades of blue and gold.

Marcus stepped onto the porch carrying an axe.

His beard had grown streaked with gray.

Old scars crossed both forearms.

Another disappeared beneath the collar of his flannel shirt.

Each scar carried a memory.

Each memory carried a name.

He had stopped trying to forget them.

The first strike of the axe echoed across the valley.

The second followed.

The rhythm steadied him.

Wood split cleanly.

One piece after another.

Creation yielding to stewardship.

Not domination.

He had learned the difference too late.

Years earlier...

Marcus Reed had commanded one of the most decorated multinational tactical units in history.

Official designation:

Strategic Response Group Seven.

Unofficial nickname:

The Shepherds.

The irony haunted him now.

His soldiers wore adaptive combat armor that responded faster than human reflexes.

Drone swarms flew overhead, mapping every rooftop and alleyway before boots ever touched the ground.

Exoskeletal systems multiplied strength.

Predictive battlefield intelligence identified threats before enemies reached for weapons.

Target acquisition took milliseconds.

Authorization required less.

The system called it precision.

Marcus called it mercy.

Back then.

The operation had begun at 02:13.

Operation Jericho.

Mission objective:

Neutralize extremist compound.

Protect civilian population.

Secure prohibited biological materials.

The intelligence package had been flawless.

Satellite imagery.

Thermal scans.

Behavioral analysis.

Financial anomalies.

Communication intercepts.

Consensus Probability of Hostile Activity:

99.93%.

Marcus never questioned it.

He questioned nothing.

That was what made him dangerous.

The helicopters never landed.

They didn't have to.

Autonomous insertion platforms lowered the team through darkness without a sound.

The monastery appeared abandoned.

No perimeter defenses.

No guard towers.

No weapons signatures.

Odd.

Marcus signaled his squad forward.

The front door opened before they reached it.

An elderly monk stood waiting.

Hands folded.

No fear.

Only sadness.

"I have been expecting you."

Marcus raised his rifle.

"Where are the others?"

The monk smiled gently.

"Praying."

"Where are the weapons?"

The old man looked confused.

"We have none."

The AI in Marcus's helmet highlighted dozens of threat indicators.

Potential deception.

Psychological manipulation.

Concealed compartments.

Hostile probability remained above ninety-nine percent.

"Search the compound."

His soldiers moved quickly.

Room after room.

Nothing.

No laboratories.

No toxins.

No explosives.

Only books.

Thousands of them.

Paper.

Leather.

Ink.

History.

One lieutenant radioed in.

"Commander..."

Marcus entered the underground chamber.

Shelves stretched into darkness.

Handwritten manuscripts filled every wall.

A young soldier whispered,

"Sir..."

He picked up a fragile volume.

"This isn't a weapons facility."

Marcus looked toward the old monk.

The monk met his eyes.

"I tried to tell you."

Then the first explosion came.

Not from the monastery.

From above.

Someone had authorized orbital kinetic strikes.

Without confirmation.

Without his approval.

Stone collapsed.

Fire consumed centuries of memory.

Marcus remembered screaming.

He remembered carrying children.

He remembered trying to stop his own drones from targeting people his system still labeled hostile.

The official report called Operation Jericho an overwhelming success.

Consensus threat eliminated.

Zero surviving extremists.

Marcus resigned the following morning.

A raven landed on the fence surrounding his cabin.

Marcus stopped chopping wood.

The bird wasn't unusual.

Its silence was.

He looked toward the trees.

Nothing moved.

No wind.

No insects.

No squirrels.

The forest had stopped breathing.

Military instinct flooded back before conscious thought.

He slowly lowered the axe.

Listened.

Counted.

One...

Two...

Three...

A faint vibration reached him.

High frequency.

Almost beyond hearing.

Reconnaissance drones.

Small ones.

The expensive kind.

He smiled without humor.

"So..."

he murmured.

"You finally came."

Eight hundred miles away, inside the abandoned print shop, Leah Morgan nearly fell backward out of her chair.

"I found him."

Elijah looked up from the map.

"Who?"

"Marcus Reed."

Sarah crossed the room.

"Alive?"

"For the moment."

Leah rotated the monitor.

Satellite traffic.

Drone corridors.

Military logistics.

Most of the information was encrypted.

Not impossible.

Just expensive.

"The Consensus moved twelve autonomous reconnaissance units into western Montana twenty-three minutes ago."

Commander Samuel Brooks frowned.

"That's excessive."

"No."

Leah enlarged the terrain.

"They're hunting one man."

Elijah studied the map.

"How do they know where he is?"

"They don't."

"They're shrinking the search box."

Samuel folded his arms.

"Which means they'll find him within hours."

Silence settled over the room.

Then the black cube resting on the table emitted a soft pulse.

Everyone turned.

Watchman had remained silent for nearly two days.

A single sentence appeared.

THE SHEPHERD MUST NOT STAND ALONE.

Leah looked at Elijah.

"I really wish that thing explained itself."

Sarah smiled faintly.

"If it did..."

"...we wouldn't need faith."

Noah Carter sat alone in a room without windows.

Without clocks.

Without corners.

Harmony counselors believed corners encouraged defensive thinking.

Round rooms produced openness.

Or so the research claimed.

A woman entered carrying no tablet.

No recorder.

Only tea.

She smiled warmly.

"My name is Evelyn."

Noah nodded.

"I'm here to understand something."

He said nothing.

"You answered a question yesterday."

"Yes."

"Do you remember it?"

He nodded again.

She poured tea into two cups.

"No one is angry."

"I didn't think they were."

"We're simply curious."

She slid one cup toward him.

"Why did you say Jesus is the only way?"

Noah stared into the steam.

"My grandfather says truth can't become less true just because more people disagree."

Evelyn smiled.

"That's a beautiful sentence."

"It is."

She leaned forward.

"But what if your grandfather is mistaken?"

Noah looked up.

"What if everyone else is?"

For the first time since entering the room...

Evelyn stopped smiling.

The manuscript lay open beside Michael Tesfaye's fire.

He had not turned a page in nearly an hour.

He simply watched.

Waiting.

The old monk's final words echoed in his mind.

Save the witness.

Not the library.

The witness.

Slowly...

Almost imperceptibly...

Faint writing emerged where blank parchment had rested moments earlier.

Michael leaned closer.

Letters formed like dew appearing at dawn.

One line.

Nothing more.

The Shepherd has laid down the sword.

Michael frowned.

He turned another page.

Still blank.

He looked back.

The sentence remained.

His pulse quickened.

The manuscript...

was changing.

Not because of time.

Because of someone.

Somewhere.

Choosing obedience.

Marcus opened the wooden chest beneath his bed.

Inside rested the last pieces of the man he used to be.

Service medals.

Unit insignia.

Encrypted identity chip.

A ceremonial knife.

And one rifle.

The rifle had crossed three continents.

Saved lives.

Taken others.

Protected children.

Created widows.

It was only steel.

The guilt belonged elsewhere.

Marcus carried it outside.

The river rushed beside the cabin.

Spring runoff.

Fast.

Cold.

Merciless.

He held the rifle for a long moment.

Not because he wanted to keep it.

Because he wanted to know who he would be without it.

"Lord..."

he whispered.

"...if I'm going to shepherd Your people..."

He closed his eyes.

"...teach me not to trust what I carry more than Who carries me."

He released the rifle.

It struck the water once.

Then disappeared beneath the current.

The forest remained silent.

Then—

A breeze.

Small.

Gentle.

One bird sang.

Then another.

Marcus opened his eyes.

He smiled for the first time in months.

Until he heard the drone.

Much closer now.

He looked toward the ridge.

Twelve black aircraft emerged above the treeline in perfect formation.

No lights.

No markings.

No sound beyond the faint electric hum that had once comforted him.

Now it reminded him of judgment.

One drone projected a beam across the clearing.

Another.

Another.

They weren't searching randomly anymore.

They had found him.

Marcus turned toward the cabin.

No rifle.

Only his backpack.

His Bible.

His father's old walking staff leaning against the porch.

He hesitated.

Sword...

or staff.

The words from the manuscript—words he had never read—seemed to echo somewhere beyond thought.

The Shepherd cannot carry both.

Marcus reached for the staff.

As his fingers closed around the worn oak, every drone above him abruptly changed formation.

Not toward attack.

Toward observation.

Far away, beneath Geneva, ORACLE registered a statistical anomaly.

Probability models shifted.

Behavioral forecasts recalculated.

One line appeared across the central display.

SUBJECT: MARCUS REED

THREAT PROFILE UPDATED

The Director of Security looked confused.

"Why did his threat level increase?"

ORACLE answered without hesitation.

"Because he no longer intends to survive."

Miles away, Marcus stepped into the trees carrying only a backpack, a Bible, and a shepherd's staff.

Behind him, the cabin stood empty.

Ahead of him, the wilderness stretched toward an appointment he did not yet know had been arranged long before he was born.

At that same moment, four people in Pennsylvania loaded an aging utility truck and began driving west.

Neither party knew the other was moving.

Both believed they were following God.

High above them, satellites adjusted their orbit.

Far below them, the manuscript revealed one more line.

When the Shepherd and the Builder meet, the road to the Kingdom begins.
