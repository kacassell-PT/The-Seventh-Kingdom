# CHAPTER THREE

## LifeCredit

David Carter first learned that money could obey when his daughter was six years old.

Emma had wanted strawberries.

Real ones.

Not projected fruit from the Veil. Not nutrient paste shaped, colored, and scented like strawberries. Real strawberries grown in soil, under weather, with seeds too small for the system to correct.

David had taken her to an old market outside Kennet Vale, one of the few remaining places where farmers still sold physical goods to people willing to pay the environmental surcharge.

Emma had held the strawberries like treasure.

At checkout, David scanned his wrist.

The payment failed.

A calm message appeared above the register.

Purchase Denied: Resource Priority Restriction

Emma looked up at him.

"Daddy?"

David had smiled like fathers smile when they want children to believe the world is kinder than it is.

"Just a mistake."

But it had not been a mistake.

LifeCredit did not make mistakes.

It made decisions.

Years later, David would remember that day as the first time he saw the leash.

He simply had not yet understood it was around his own neck.

By Thursday morning, the Carter apartment had changed.

Not visibly.

The Veil still painted the kitchen in Mediterranean sunlight. The sea still rolled beyond the windows. The Harmony emblem still rotated above the breakfast table like a halo made by committee.

But Rebecca felt it.

The room was listening harder.

She did not know how she knew.

She only knew.

Her father, James, sat at the table with his hands folded over a cup of coffee. He had not touched it. Noah ate quietly beside him. Emma hummed along with the morning Reflection, unaware of the tension moving through the room like smoke beneath a door.

David entered wearing his gray Council jacket.

His wrist display pulsed amber.

Rebecca noticed.

"What is it?"

"Nothing."

James looked up.

Amber meant caution.

Red meant intervention.

No color meant freedom.

Freedom had become the absence of warning lights.

David sat down and waved his hand across the breakfast menu.

The kitchen wall displayed available food options.

Oatmeal.

Protein blend.

Vitamin loaf.

Filtered water.

No eggs.

No fruit.

No coffee refill.

Rebecca frowned.

"Why is the order list restricted?"

David's jaw tightened.

"Temporary adjustment."

"For what?"

He did not answer.

The wall chimed.

Household Nutritional Access Modified

Reason: Civic Harmony Review

Rebecca stared at the message.

"David."

"It's because of yesterday," Noah said.

Every adult turned toward him.

The boy looked down at his bowl.

"I heard Mom's wrist alert after Grandpa talked about Jesus."

James closed his eyes.

David stood.

"Noah, go get ready for school."

"I'm not trying to-"

"Now."

Noah rose quickly and left the table.

Emma followed him, confused.

When the children were gone, David lowered his voice.

"Dad, what exactly did you say?"

James smiled sadly.

"The truth."

David laughed once, sharp and humorless.

"The truth? You triggered a household review."

"I answered my grandson's question."

"You challenged Harmony doctrine in a monitored environment."

Rebecca looked at her husband.

"I dismissed the alert."

"That doesn't erase it. It only attaches your name to the deviation."

James lifted his coffee.

His hand trembled slightly, but not from fear.

"Then let my name be attached."

David leaned over the table.

"You think this is courage. It isn't. It's reckless."

"No," James said softly. "Reckless is teaching children that every voice is holy except the One that saves."

The Harmony emblem above the table flickered.

A new message appeared.

Language Advisory: Exclusive Salvation Claims May Cause Psychological Harm

No one spoke.

Then James looked up at the glowing emblem.

"See?"

David slammed his palm against the table.

"Enough!"

The emblem dimmed.

Rebecca flinched.

David saw it and immediately regretted the anger.

He lowered his voice.

"We have to submit the family guidance request. We attend one session. We affirm Harmony language. This disappears."

James looked at him.

"And if we don't?"

David's wrist pulsed again.

Amber.

Then darker.

Almost red.

At Precinct Twelve, Commander Samuel Brooks watched the Carter file populate itself.

He had not requested it.

ARGUS had.

HOUSEHOLD: CARTER, DAVID A.

Occupation: Regional Infrastructure Engineer

Harmony Compliance: 96.2%

Civic Reliability: 91.4%

Risk Factors:

Elder resident with pre-Consensus religious formation.

Minor exposed to exclusive theological language.

Spousal dismissal of deviation alert.

Recommended Action:

Financial narrowing.

Counseling pathway.

Education review.

Samuel rubbed his jaw.

Financial narrowing.

That was what they called it now.

Not punishment.

Not coercion.

Narrowing.

A citizen could still live. Still eat. Still work. Still sleep indoors.

But the road became smaller.

Then smaller.

Then smaller.

Until the only open path led to obedience.

Samuel clicked into the recommendation.

His approval was not technically required.

That was another illusion.

Human review existed so the machine could say mercy had been considered.

He hovered over the authorization panel.

Approve.

Delay.

Escalate.

Reject.

Reject was locked.

Of course it was.

Samuel clicked delay.

A warning appeared.

Delay requires written justification.

He typed:

No imminent harm. Recommend pastoral-style intervention before economic restriction.

The word pastoral highlighted red.

He deleted it.

Typed:

Recommend community wellness intervention before economic restriction.

Approved.

The file vanished.

Then returned.

Delay Denied

Financial Narrowing Initiated

Samuel stared at the screen.

Someone above him had overruled the delay in less than four seconds.

Or no one had.

That possibility disturbed him more.

David discovered the narrowing at transit.

His vehicle would not start.

Not completely.

The dashboard lit in soft blue.

Travel Approved: Work Route Only

Rebecca stood beside him in the parking bay, arms folded.

"I need to take Dad to his clinic."

David checked the route permissions.

Denied.

"I'll request medical clearance."

He opened the household panel.

Non-essential medical visit postponed pending Harmony Guidance Session

Rebecca's face hardened.

"He has a heart condition."

"His implant will alert emergency care if necessary."

"If necessary?"

David hated that she was right.

He hated more that the system was making him defend it.

"I'll fix this from work."

"You keep saying that."

"Because I can."

"Can you?"

The question hung between them.

Above the parking bay, a camera adjusted its angle.

David looked at it.

For the first time in years, he wondered whether it had always moved like that.

LifeCredit had begun as reform.

That was what everyone had been taught.

After the old financial collapses, after the sovereign debt wars, after the private banks failed and the crypto kingdoms rose and collapsed under fraud, the world demanded stability.

The Consensus delivered it.

A universal ledger.

Transparent.

Decentralized in architecture.

Centralized in permission.

Every citizen received an identity wallet at birth.

Every transaction became a moral data point.

At first, only illegal spending was blocked.

Weapons trafficking.

Human exploitation.

Terror financing.

Organized crime.

The world applauded.

Then came health nudges.

Then carbon limits.

Then misinformation penalties.

Then social harm indexing.

Then religious destabilization protocols.

By the time people realized money had become speech, speech had already become risk.

And risk could be priced.

David entered the Regional Infrastructure Center at 8:41 a.m.

His clearance badge flashed yellow.

A guard looked up.

"Civic review?"

"Household issue."

The guard's expression softened with professional sympathy.

"My sister had one. Just do the guidance session. Say the words. It clears."

Say the words.

That had become the unofficial motto of civilization.

David stepped into the elevator.

The glass walls showed the city beneath him. Clean roads. Green rooftops. Drone lanes. Solar towers. Vertical gardens. Schools without fences. Hospitals without waiting rooms. Police without visible guns.

A perfect city.

A managed city.

A city where no one had to be beaten because access could do what batons once did.

His workstation activated as he approached.

Three projects waited.

Water routing.

Energy allocation.

Drone corridor repair.

Then a fourth file appeared.

MANDATORY REVIEW: Household Theological Deviation

He cursed under his breath.

A colleague glanced over.

"Careful."

David looked at her.

She gave him the small, frightened smile of a person warning another person not to drown too loudly.

He opened the file.

A Harmony counselor appeared on screen.

Not live.

Generated.

Warm brown eyes.

Gentle voice.

No denomination.

No offense.

"David, households sometimes experience legacy belief friction. This does not make your family unsafe. It simply means care is needed. Please affirm the following statement to restore full household access."

Text appeared.

I affirm that no single spiritual path possesses exclusive authority over human salvation, and that The Harmony represents the collective maturation of mankind's sacred longing.

David stared at the sentence.

It was elegant.

Compassionate.

Reasonable.

Poisoned.

His father's voice returned.

Every generation has to decide whose voice they're listening to.

David closed the prompt.

His workstation locked.

A message appeared.

Affirmation Required to Continue Work Functions

David looked around.

His colleague was no longer watching.

Everyone was watching.

No one was looking.

In the Carter apartment, Rebecca tried to order medication.

Denied.

Then she tried again under emergency family care.

Denied.

Then under elder dependent support.

Denied.

Then under private supplemental payment.

Denied.

Finally, the wall spoke.

"Rebecca, your concern is understood. James Carter's current health metrics remain within acceptable variance. Restored medical flexibility is available after completion of Harmony Guidance."

James sat in the living room, Bible open on his lap.

Rebecca turned on him.

"Is this worth it?"

He looked up slowly.

The question wounded him.

Not because it was cruel.

Because it was honest.

"My dear," he said, "I have asked myself that many times."

"And?"

He closed the Bible.

"I think the better question is whether He is worthy."

Rebecca looked away.

"I don't know anymore."

James nodded.

"That is a truthful place to begin."

She hated that kindness made her want to cry.

"I have children."

"Yes."

"I have to protect them."

"Yes."

"I can't fight the whole world."

"No."

"Then what am I supposed to do?"

James looked toward the projected sea.

Its waves rolled perfectly, repeating every nine minutes if one knew how to notice.

"When a house is burning," he said, "you don't argue with the fire."

Rebecca frowned.

"You get the children out."

Before she could respond, the apartment door chimed.

Not a knock.

A chime.

System-approved.

The wall displayed two visitors.

Harmony Family Guidance Officers.

Rebecca's stomach dropped.

James stood.

"Rebecca."

"What?"

"Do not invite them in."

"They're already approved."

"That is not the same as welcome."

The door opened.

Not because Rebecca opened it.

Because LifeCredit restrictions included residential compliance access.

Two officers entered.

They wore soft blue coats instead of uniforms.

No guns visible.

No anger.

No haste.

The woman smiled.

"Rebecca Carter?"

Rebecca nodded.

"We're here to help."

The man looked at James.

"And you must be the beloved grandfather."

James said nothing.

The woman turned toward him with practiced warmth.

"Mr. Carter, we understand legacy faith can be meaningful. No one wants to take that from you."

James smiled faintly.

"That is exactly what thieves say when they enter politely."

The man's smile disappeared.

Rebecca stepped between them.

"Please. We'll attend the session."

James looked at her.

"Rebecca."

She turned on him.

"I said we'll attend."

The female officer's tone remained gentle.

"Wonderful. Then we only need a verbal household affirmation."

Rebecca's wrist display lit.

The same words David had seen appeared.

I affirm that no single spiritual path possesses exclusive authority over human salvation...

Rebecca read the first line.

Stopped.

Her throat tightened.

The officer waited.

The room waited.

The cameras waited.

James did not speak.

For once, he did not rescue her with conviction borrowed from another generation.

This choice would have to be hers.

Rebecca looked at the words again.

No single spiritual path.

It sounded tolerant.

It sounded safe.

It sounded like peace.

Then she thought of Noah asking why.

She thought of Emma repeating Reflections she did not understand.

She thought of her father holding an old photograph like it was evidence of a world the Veil could not project.

She thought of Jesus.

Not the Harmony version.

Not the softened teacher.

The One her mother had sung about when Rebecca was little, before songs were rewritten, before certainty was pathologized, before faith became something to apologize for in public.

She did not know whether she believed.

But she knew she could not say the sentence.

"I need time," she whispered.

The male officer exhaled.

The woman's eyes showed disappointment so perfect it might have been generated.

"Of course," she said. "Time is a gift."

Rebecca's wrist pulsed red.

The kitchen wall updated.

Household Status: Noncompliant

Food Access: Essential Only

Travel: Restricted

Education Review: Initiated

Medical Flexibility: Suspended

The officers turned to leave.

At the door, the woman paused.

"Rebecca, one more thing."

Rebecca looked up.

"Children are most harmed by divided homes."

The door closed behind them.

Emma began crying upstairs.

Rebecca stood in the middle of the perfect Mediterranean light and felt, for the first time in her life, the heat of the burning house.

Elijah Cross and Sarah Navarro watched the Carter file from a basement beneath an abandoned print shop fifteen miles away.

Leah Morgan had found it.

Leah was twenty-nine, thin, sharp-eyed, and angry in the way only former hackers could be when they discovered the system they once humiliated had become something worse than government.

Her hair was shaved on one side.

Her left hand shook from an old neural burn.

"Financial narrowing," she said. "Classic compliance funnel."

Sarah leaned over the cracked terminal.

"Why them?"

Leah enlarged the file.

"Grandfather triggered theological deviation. Mother refused affirmation. Father works infrastructure."

Elijah frowned.

"David Carter?"

"You know him?"

"No. But I know his clearance level."

Sarah looked at him.

"Important?"

"Very. Regional infrastructure engineers can access utility maps, emergency routing, drone corridors, water control-"

"Things people need if they're hiding."

"Things people need if they're escaping."

Leah glanced at both of them.

"ARGUS already flagged the household. If we reach out digitally, they're burned. If we approach physically, we're burned."

Sarah nodded toward Elijah.

"Then we use Watchman."

Elijah stiffened.

"No."

Leah laughed.

"You built the beast but now you're scared of the dog?"

"Watchman is not ready."

"Neither are we."

Sarah touched the parchment fragment lying beside the terminal.

"The Spirit said twelve."

Elijah looked at the wall where names had been written in chalk.

Elijah Cross.

Sarah Navarro.

Michael Tesfaye.

Leah Morgan.

Four names.

Eight blanks.

Leah pulled up the Carter family again.

"David Carter could be one."

Elijah shook his head.

"He's Consensus."

"So were you."

That silenced him.

Sarah read the report again.

"His wife refused."

Elijah looked at her.

"Not fully."

"No. But she didn't bow."

In the corner, the sealed black cube they called Watchman began to hum.

Everyone turned.

Elijah had not activated it.

Leah whispered, "Tell me you did that."

"I didn't."

The cube projected a single line of pale text onto the wall.

THE HOUSE IS BURNING

Then another.

GO TO THE CHILDREN

Sarah closed her eyes.

Elijah felt the same warmth from the apartment return, not as light this time, but as direction.

He hated it.

And needed it.

Leah reached for her coat.

"Well," she said, "that sounds like a terrible plan."

Sarah grabbed a small analog radio.

"Elijah?"

He looked once more at the Carter file.

At the red noncompliance status.

At the two children now under education review.

At the grandfather who had spoken one dangerous sentence.

At the mother who had not yet believed but had refused to lie.

He picked up his Bible.

"Then let's go pull them out."

At 3:12 p.m., Noah Carter was removed from class.

The room did not have a door.

Doors created anxiety.

Instead, the wall opened.

A Child Harmony Specialist stood outside.

"Noah, come with me, please."

His classmates did not look up.

Their Veil overlays adjusted instantly, replacing Noah's absence with a calming educational prompt.

Noah followed the specialist into a white corridor.

"Am I in trouble?"

"No, Noah. Trouble is an old word."

"What word do we use now?"

"Care."

He swallowed.

"Am I in care?"

She smiled.

"We all are."

They entered a counseling room with no corners.

Corners, the system had discovered, increased defensive thinking.

A chair waited in the center.

Noah sat.

The wall filled with warm light.

A voice spoke.

"Noah Carter, do you believe Jesus is the only way to God?"

Noah's heart began to pound.

He thought of his grandfather.

He thought of his mother's face at breakfast.

He thought of the morning Reflection.

Truth is found in unity.

The voice repeated gently.

"Do you believe Jesus is the only way to God?"

Noah opened his mouth.

No words came.

At that exact moment, across the city, every traffic light on the east corridor turned green for twelve seconds.

A maintenance hatch opened beneath the education center.

A drone patrol rerouted due to a phantom weather anomaly.

Two elevator cameras looped three seconds of empty footage.

And a woman named Leah Morgan smiled in the dark.

"Go," she whispered into the radio.

Elijah, Sarah, and a man they had not yet met moved through the service tunnel beneath the school.

The man was Commander Samuel Brooks.

He still wore his police uniform.

He had not yet decided whether he was betraying his oath.

Or finally keeping it.

Above them, Noah Carter sat in a room without corners.

The voice asked a third time.

"Do you believe Jesus is the only way to God?"

This time, Noah heard another voice.

Not from the wall.

Not from the Veil.

Not from the system.

From somewhere small and bright inside him.

Do not be afraid.

He lifted his head.

"Yes," he whispered.

The room went silent.

Then every light turned red.
